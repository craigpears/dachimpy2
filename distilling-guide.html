The rules in brief

Just enough to follow the rest. Puzzle Pirates' own tutorial teaches the basics properly.

The pieces

Ten columns of bubbles, set like a honeycomb, tall and short in turn. Each bubble touches four others on its diagonals, never the ones straight above or below it. Whites are the rum. Browns and blacks are junk. Spice never moves and is worth a lot if it goes up. A burnt white moves like a white but spoils whatever it's in.

A piece's weight says which way its column goes when it leaves; its points count only if it goes up.

The swap rule

Two touching bubbles swap only when the lower one beats the upper one, like rock, paper, scissors:

So a white rises through blacks and sinks through browns, a brown rises through whites, a black rises through browns. A swap can't be undone straight away: the pair's now the wrong way round. Hold a bubble and drag, and it keeps swapping as long as it can, so a whole move is one smooth sweep.

The furnace

Every 15 seconds the right-hand column leaves. More blacks than whites and it goes down into the furnace; otherwise up into the flask. You can burn it sooner yourself (X, or right-click). Then the board slides right and a new column comes in on the left.

A white that goes down the furnace is lost, and every second one burned comes back later as a burnt white.

Crystal Clears and chains

A column of only whites sent up, spice and all, is a Crystal Clear. Each one straight after another scores 4 more for every one before it: that's a chain, written CC^2, CC^3 and on. Anything else that leaves, up or down, breaks it. Columns as the game judges them:

The flask's full at 100 pieces, about twelve columns, and that's the batch. On a chain of 12 or more it carries on until the chain breaks.

Setting a target

A Crystal Clear to aim for

Before you start, pick the chain you're after. Not twelve: start with one.

The target says how long you build. You bank whites on the left until there's enough for that chain, then send them up one Crystal Clear after another. When the chain breaks with whites still banked on the right, keep sending them up: a new chain. Once they're gone, go back to building for the next. Once you make your target every time, aim one higher. The longer the chain, the more whites you bank first and the more techniques it takes. The den's practice players play at these same levels.

  • CC^1

    One column of whites, sent up. The simple techniques are all it takes.

  • CC^2

    Two in a row. Start sorting the junk: blacks up, browns down, some kept back for later.

  • CC^3

    Three. Junk carried sideways along a row in one go.

  • CC^5

    Five. Pairs of junk working together, and pieces hooked round corners, for when pieces get scarce.

  • CC^8

    Eight. Pairs looped up and down a nearly white board, and lines of browns pulled along.

At Helium's still: a goal, and a plan for it

Helium sets each batch a goal: at least your best chain plus one, so a Crystal Clear 1 to start, and more if you can. Once the chain has reached the goal, the batch ends as soon as a column that isn't Crystal Clear leaves. Then it's a fresh board, with the same goal or a bigger one.

Their plan for it comes a step at a time, shown on your own board:

  1. Clear the junk columns. The columns that will burn are shaded red: get every white out of them first.
  2. Bank the whites. If there aren't enough on the board for the goal yet, gather them on the left.
  3. Build the Crystal Clear. The column it's built in is shaded gold: fill it with whites and get the junk out.
  4. Send it up. Let the furnace take it, the junk ahead of it burned first. Then the next column, if you're going for more.

The arrows show plain moves first: one short drag, one of a few tricks that are each the swap rule seen once (the climb, a white up through blacks; the sink, a white down through browns; the lift, a brown up through whites; the drop, a black down through whites; the weave, a white up and down in one drag), with why it works. Longer moves and Helium's own techniques come only with bigger goals. When nothing plain helps yet, a short drag first makes way, the two numbered.

The tricks side by side, each one drag on Helium's lesson board and its rule in their words. Helium plays with the same ones: every drag they make is one of these tricks, or a run of them, and each has a goal, so none is a half move for nothing.

Rules of thumb

Three to keep in mind all game, whatever you're doing.

Never a white down the fire

The one thing to know. A white burned is a white lost, and every second one comes back as a burnt white. Burn one in the first few columns and the game's as good as over. Played well, you never burn a white at all.

Helium's opening board: this column, with its white, goes down the furnace, and the still goes bang.

The danger low by the fire first

Whites in the column next to burn that can't get out on their own, low down, boxed in or behind a spice, are the hardest to reach, so start on them first, before easy work anywhere else. The top of the board is safe and easy; the bottom by the fire is where whites get burned. If nothing gets one out in a single move, don't freeze: set it up, a piece moved into place first (a brown low in the middle, say), and the drag it opens next.

Nothing walled in at the edges or by a spice

In the three columns by the fire, a white on the top or bottom edge, or beside a spice, has few ways out, and they close as the board slides. Race junk into its place while you can, from far off if need be, and never make a move that blocks its way out. A white with spice on both its ways left is walled off for good: spice never moves, so it can only drift right till it burns. Never let a white into such a pocket.

Reading the board

Before each move, look at the board as a whole and ask how it's doing. Not one piece at a time: which whites near the fire are in danger, and which way trouble is heading. Helium does this every move.

One white at a time. For each white in the three columns by the fire: how trapped it is (on the top row with blacks round it, beside a spice, walled in), how many swaps it would take any junk to reach it and take its place, and how soon its column burns. A white at the top with blacks round it comes before one lower down that junk can still flow to. The higher up and the more boxed in, the sooner it needs help.

Patterns. Some trouble is a group of pieces: blacks stuck at the bottom and browns at the top among the whites, with no way out on their own; how many pieces are stuck; how many columns the free junk can still fill; the bottom of the V left unsorted; and the two right-hand columns not sorted blacks over browns. That pair is the ladder the zigzag climbs, and when the right-hand column burns it's gone. The closer it gets to the fire unsorted, the bigger the risk.

From those, the board is in one of four states, and each state has a plan:

  • Healthy: nothing at risk. Quick, simple moves: blacks down, browns up. Once the whites are mostly left, move the junk in blocks.
  • At risk: a white near the fire in danger, or the ladder about to be lost. Free the trapped whites first, the worst first. Fill the gaps by the fire with the quickest junk moves. Sort the ladder before its column goes.
  • Cracked: a lot of junk stranded among the whites. Stop filling the columns and fix the cracks. The browns in the middle are your tools for getting the blacks out, so don't send them off to the right.
  • Damage control: too many whites by the fire for the time and junk left. Fill the gaps however you can, the quickest first, then clear the stranded junk out of the way, and go for the best chain still in reach.

A game, phase by phase

A game runs through four phases. The board says which one you're in, and each wants different moves. You'll be back in the first every time a column burns and brings a new one onto the fire's side. Under each phase are its own moves, simplest first; the tag says the first target that needs one.

Every board is real, and every move is one that's played on it. An arrow is one drag: pick the piece up at the dot and follow the line to the arrowhead without letting go. Each drag has its own colour, numbered in order when there's more than one. Shaded columns are the ones it's about; ringed bubbles are the ones to watch.

1The fire's side

The fire's side

When: any white in the three columns next to the fire. They burn first, so every white in them goes left before anything else.

This is Helium's own step one, on their lesson board: the whites ringed, and the drags they use to walk them out, in order.

The sweep

At the start there's plenty of junk and plenty of time. Grab a brown or a black and drag it right as far as it'll go, then the next, one after another, hardly stopping to look. Rough is fine: speed matters more than neatness this early.

2Filling the junk columns

Filling the junk columns

When: the fire's side is clear of white. Now fill the right-hand columns with junk, from the fire in, one column at a time, each finished before the next. Sweep the junk over in long rough drags; there's no need to be neat yet.

The same lesson board once step one's done: shaded, the columns that are junk now and the one being filled.

The diamond

A black from the left falls down-right across the whites, then climbs up through the browns on the right to join the blacks at the top. A brown goes the other way round: up across the whites, down through the blacks. So the junk columns fill black above and brown below, a diamond on its side.

Turning the right side over

Blacks taken up the right-hand side, zigzagging up through the browns to the top while the browns sink. Then whatever comes over from the left can hook straight in.

3Blocks and the V

Blocks

When: the whites are mostly on the left and colour's down to half the board or so. Stop fiddling with single pieces. Move the junk in blocks, browns up together, blacks down together, and wrap it round the whites in a V: the right-hand columns and a row along the bottom.

Ringed, the corner tools: a brown kept at the bottom-left and a black at the top-left, for later.

The leapfrog

Sideways along a row: a black dragged along a row of browns, up into each and down past it, all in one drag (or a brown along a row of blacks). It needs plenty of pieces about, so it's a mid-game move.

4The chain

The chain

When: enough whites are banked for your target. Now it all turns round: whites go right, to fill each column before it leaves, and the junk piles up on the left. Every column has to go up Crystal Clear, so nothing more can burn. If the chain breaks, the whites left on the right are still the bank: send them up as a new chain, and never fill a white column with junk to burn it.

Shaded, a column of whites ready to go up. The two moves after it are for longer chains: a Crystal Clear or two never needs them.

The hook

A black dragged down through the whites hooks up past a brown, pushing the brown down, and then the brown's dragged on. For when pieces get scarce, and for hooking junk in through the V in one big sweep.

Pulling a line of browns

A black at the front of a line of browns climbs past them, every brown dropping a place, then loops back round to do it again: the line pulled along a tile at a time.
